# Customer problem tests

Choose tests for the actual segment and decision. Keep hypotheses separate from evidence. The same situation can contain a real customer problem and a weak proposed business.

| Test | What to inspect or ask | Competing explanation / next check |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Specific incident | A recent concrete occurrence, the surrounding workflow, who was affected and what happened | A rehearsed general complaint may lack a repeated or material underlying event; inspect a task log or walk through a real occurrence |
| Root cause | Sequence of events and constraints creating the outcome; what changes when one cause is removed | The proposed product may treat a symptom, move work elsewhere, or address a different stakeholder's problem |
| Consequence | Delay, lost revenue, effort, quality, risk or desired experience, with period and provenance | Claimed hours saved may not become cash saved; an estimated risk is not an observed annual loss |
| Frequency/exposure | How often the job arises and when stakes become high; event-based rather than arbitrary monthly use | Rare but severe compliance/safety events or episodic buying can still matter; demand appropriate exposure/impact evidence |
| Priority and trigger | What action the customer took, what was displaced, who sponsored it and why now | The problem may exist but rank below other needs; look for a budget/deadline/change that alters priority |
| Current alternative | Actual tools, manual work, service provider, internal workaround or deliberate inaction | A seemingly clumsy alternative may be cheap, trusted, flexible or adequate; quantify the tradeoff rather than ridicule it |
| Buyer and budget | User, beneficiary, sponsor, purchaser, approval process and relevant resources | The affected user may not control spending; a missing existing category budget does not eliminate possible reallocation |
| Switching/adoption | Integration, data access, liability, habit, trust, retraining, procurement and total change cost | Rejection can reflect adoption burden or a weak solution, not absence of need; test these explanations separately |
| Commitment and behavior | Actual effort, data access, operational change, paid terms, follow-through and repeat behavior when available | Polite feedback, founder relationships, incentives or refundable commitments may inflate apparent demand |
| Segment consistency | Differences across roles, organization size, urgency trigger, geography and existing alternatives | A strong pain in one niche may not generalize; narrow the hypothesis rather than average conflicting segments |

## Quantifying consequences without inventing ROI

For time/effort, retain the workflow denominator, task frequency, staff involved and sampling period. An illustrative labor-cost equivalent may use hours × a supplied fully loaded rate, but is not realized savings unless resources or productive output actually change. Do not add overlapping time, salary and revenue benefits as if independent.

For risk, distinguish observed incidents, severity/exposure evidence and assumed probability. Only calculate expected loss when the probability and impact inputs have a defensible basis; show their sensitivity. Verify applicable legal requirements through current authoritative sources when central to a live claim. Do not invent penalties to create urgency.

For consumer or aspirational products, assess the desired experience, opportunity, convenience, identity or creative outcome through behavior and appropriate alternatives. Willingness to allocate attention, time, money or effort can be relevant. Engagement alone does not establish sustainable monetization; route that question to the business/venture assessment.

## Problem versus solution

Use a two-axis assessment:

- Strong problem evidence + weak solution evidence: keep the problem thesis, investigate feasibility, differentiation or adoption friction.
- Weak problem evidence + strong demo: investigate whether the product serves a valuable job before extrapolating demand.
- Strong evidence for both: examine repeatability, business model, market and scale; don't claim those are proven.
- Weak evidence for both: narrow the customer hypothesis and design discovery; don't invent certainty to salvage the pitch.

Customers can have difficulty describing a latent need or imagining a new product. Test observed behavior and the effects of a usable prototype where justified; hypothetical survey answers alone cannot settle that question in either direction.
